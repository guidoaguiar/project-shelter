# The Rule of Threes
tags: priorities, triage, planning, mindset

A rough ordering of what kills fastest, used to decide what to do first when everything seems urgent.

## The rule

A person can survive roughly:

- **3 minutes** without air or with severe bleeding
- **3 hours** without shelter in harsh weather
- **3 days** without water
- **3 weeks** without food

These are rules of thumb, not guarantees. Cold water, heat, injury and exertion shorten every one of them.

## What it means in practice

1. Deal with immediate threats to life: airway, bleeding, getting out of danger.
2. Protect body temperature. Exposure kills far more lost people than hunger does.
3. Secure water before you are thirsty.
4. Food comes last. Do not burn 800 calories chasing 200.

> **NOTE** Most people in trouble die of exposure or injury within the first two days. Shelter and warmth are almost always the first job.

# STOP: What To Do When Lost
tags: lost, panic, mindset, rescue

The moment you realise you are lost is the most dangerous one, because the urge to hurry makes everything worse. Use STOP.

## The drill

1. **Stop.** Sit down. Drink water. You are not more lost than you were five minutes ago.
2. **Think.** When were you last sure of your position? How long ago? What direction have you travelled since?
3. **Observe.** Weather, daylight left, terrain, your gear, your condition and the group's.
4. **Plan.** Decide whether to stay or move, then act deliberately.

## Stay or go

Stay put if someone knows your route and will raise the alarm. Searchers start at your last known point; every step away makes their job harder.

Move only if nobody knows where you are, you are certain of a direction to safety, or your position is dangerous. If you move, leave a note or marker showing your direction and time.

## Estimate remaining daylight

Hold your arm straight out and stack fingers between the horizon and the sun. Each finger is roughly 15 minutes at mid latitudes; four fingers is about an hour. Begin building shelter with at least two hours of light left.

# The 72-Hour Kit
tags: kit, bugout, go bag, checklist, preparedness

A grab-and-go bag that covers one person for three days, long enough for most evacuations or for services to return.

## Contents

| Need | Carry |
|---|---|
| Water | 3 L in bottles, a filter or purification tablets, metal cup |
| Shelter | Tarp or bivy bag, cordage (15 m), foil blanket, spare socks, hat, rain layer |
| Fire | Lighter, ferro rod, waterproofed tinder |
| Food | 6000 kcal that needs no cooking: bars, nuts, dried fruit, tinned fish |
| Medical | Pressure bandage, tourniquet, gauze, tape, personal medication for 7 days |
| Light | Headlamp and spare batteries |
| Signal | Whistle, mirror, charged power bank, radio |
| Tools | Fixed-blade knife, multitool, duct tape, work gloves |
| Navigation | Paper map of your area, compass |
| Papers | Copies of ID, contacts written on paper, cash in small notes |

## Upkeep

- Check twice a year, when the clocks change. Rotate food, water, batteries and medication.
- Keep it where you can reach it in the dark.
- Weight target: under 20% of your body weight. A kit you cannot carry is not a kit.

# Home Blackout Protocol
tags: power outage, blackout, generator, carbon monoxide, food safety

How to run a household safely through a multi-day power cut.

## First hour

1. Check whether the fault is yours alone (breakers) or area-wide.
2. Unplug sensitive electronics to protect them from the surge when power returns.
3. Fill containers and the bathtub with water while pressure lasts.
4. Keep fridge and freezer doors shut.

## Food

- A closed refrigerator keeps food safe for about **4 hours**.
- A full freezer holds for about **48 hours**, a half-full one about **24**.
- Discard perishable food that has been above 4 °C (40 °F) for more than 2 hours. When in doubt, throw it out.
- Eat fridge food first, then freezer food, then dry stores.

## Heat and light

- Live in one room. Close it off, hang blankets over doors and windows.
- Prefer battery lights to candles. Never leave a flame unattended.

> **WARNING** Carbon monoxide has no smell. Never run a generator, charcoal grill or camp stove indoors or in a garage. Keep generators outside, at least 6 m (20 ft) from any door, window or vent, and keep a battery CO alarm inside.

# Field Sanitation
tags: hygiene, latrine, toilet, waste, disease, handwashing

When plumbing fails, disease spreads through waste and unwashed hands. Good sanitation prevents more illness than any medicine you carry.

## Latrines

- Site at least **60 m (200 ft)** from any water source, camp and kitchen, and downhill of where you collect water.
- Short stay: cat hole 15 to 20 cm deep, filled in after use.
- Longer stay: trench about 30 cm wide and 60 cm deep. Cover each use with soil or ash. Fill in when within 30 cm of the surface.

## At home without sewage

Use the twin-bucket system: one bucket for urine, one for faeces, each with a seat. Cover faeces after every use with sawdust, shredded paper or dry soil. Keep the lid on. Bury the contents away from water, or store sealed until services return.

## Hands

Wash with soap and water after the toilet and before handling food. If water is short, a tippy-tap (a hanging jug tipped by a foot lever) washes hands with very little. Wood ash works as a scouring substitute when there is no soap.

## Rubbish

Burn what burns, bury the rest away from camp. Keep food waste away from sleeping areas to avoid drawing animals.
