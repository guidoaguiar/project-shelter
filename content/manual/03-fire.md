# Fire Fundamentals
tags: fire, tinder, kindling, fuel, fire lay, wet weather

Fire gives warmth, safe water, cooked food, light, signal and morale. Most failed fires fail in preparation, not ignition.

## The triangle

Fire needs **heat, fuel and oxygen**. Take one away and it dies. Most smothered fires have too much fuel packed too tightly.

## Gather before you strike

| Stage | Size | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Tinder | Fine and fluffy: dry grass, birch bark, shaved wood, lint | Two fists |
| Kindling | Pencil lead up to thumb thickness | An armful |
| Fuel | Wrist to leg thickness | A large stack, then double it |

Wood that snaps cleanly is dry. Wood that bends is not.

## Fire lays

- **Teepee:** kindling leaned around tinder. Fast, hot, good for starting.
- **Log cabin:** stacked square around a teepee. Steady and good for cooking.
- **Long fire:** two logs side by side, burning along their length. Warms a sleeper.
- **Dakota hole:** a fire pit with a second tunnel feeding air. Efficient, low smoke, hidden light.

## In the wet

- Take dead wood that is still standing, not lying on the ground.
- Split it: the core is dry even after days of rain.
- Carve feather sticks, thin curls left attached to a dry stick.
- Fatwood (resin-soaked pine from stumps and knots) and birch bark burn when damp.
- Build on a platform of sticks and shield the flame until it is established.

## Safety

Clear the ground to bare soil for a metre around. Keep fires away from overhanging branches and tents. Drown, stir and feel the ashes before you leave.

# Sparks: Ferro Rod, Flint and Steel
tags: fire, ferro rod, flint, steel, char cloth, ignition

Lighters run out and matches get wet. Spark methods keep working for years.

## Ferrocerium rod

Produces sparks near 3000 °C and works when wet.

1. Prepare a nest of the finest tinder you can make.
2. Rest the tip of the rod in the tinder.
3. Hold the scraper still and **pull the rod back** against it. This keeps the sparks in place and stops you knocking the tinder apart.
4. Use slow, firm strokes rather than fast flicks.

Good tinder for a ferro rod: birch bark shavings, fatwood dust, cotton wool with petroleum jelly, dry grass rubbed to fluff.

## Flint and steel

The older method. A high-carbon steel striker throws cooler sparks off a sharp edge of flint, chert or quartz. These sparks need char cloth to catch.

1. Lay a piece of char cloth on top of the flint, at the edge.
2. Strike the steel downward along the edge, shaving off sparks.
3. When the cloth shows a glowing orange spot, fold it into a tinder bundle.
4. Blow gently and steadily until it flames.

## Making char cloth

1. Cut pure cotton or linen into 5 cm squares.
2. Put them in a small tin with a pinhole in the lid.
3. Set the tin in coals. Smoke will jet from the hole.
4. When the smoke stops, remove the tin and let it cool **before** opening.

The cloth should be black and flexible, not brown or crumbling. Keep it dry.

# Friction Fire: The Bow Drill
tags: fire, bow drill, friction, primitive, ember

Fire from wood alone. It is hard, and it should be learned at home, not in an emergency.

## Parts

- **Hearth board:** flat, about 2 cm thick, of soft dry wood.
- **Spindle:** straight, thumb-thick, 20 to 25 cm long, of the same or similar wood.
- **Bow:** a slightly curved branch, arm length, with a strong cord.
- **Bearing block:** a hard piece with a socket, to press down on the spindle. Grease it with oil or a green leaf.

Good woods: willow, poplar, cottonwood, basswood (lime), cedar. Test with a thumbnail: it should dent but not crumble. Avoid resinous wood. Everything must be bone dry.

## Method

1. Carve a shallow dimple near the edge of the board and burn it in with a few strokes.
2. Cut a notch, a one-eighth pie slice, from the edge to just short of the centre of the dimple. The dust collects here.
3. Put a dry leaf or bark chip under the notch.
4. Kneel with one foot on the board, the bearing hand braced against your shin.
5. Loop the spindle once in the cord. Start with long, smooth strokes and light pressure.
6. As smoke builds, add speed and pressure for 10 to 20 more strokes.
7. Stop. If the dust pile keeps smoking on its own, you have an ember. Let it grow for a few breaths.
8. Tip it into a tinder bundle and blow it into flame.

## When it fails

- Squeaking and polished surfaces: add a pinch of sand, press harder.
- Brown dust instead of black: more speed and pressure.
- Cord slipping: tighten the bow or roughen the spindle.
