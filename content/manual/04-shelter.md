# Choosing a Shelter Site
tags: shelter, campsite, site selection, hazards

A good site does half the work of the shelter. Spend ten minutes choosing it.

## The five W's

- **Wind:** find natural windbreaks. Avoid exposed ridges and the cold air that pools in valley bottoms.
- **Water:** close enough to fetch, but at least 60 m away and above the flood line. Never camp in a dry wash.
- **Widowmakers:** look up. Dead branches and leaning dead trees fall without warning.
- **Wood:** building material and fuel within easy carry.
- **Wildlife:** avoid game trails, ant nests, wasp nests and standing water that breeds insects.

## Ground

Choose flat ground with a slight slope so rain drains away. A site halfway up a slope is warmer than both the valley floor and the hilltop. Face the opening away from the prevailing wind, and toward the morning sun if you can.

## Timing

Start with at least two hours of daylight left. A shelter built in the dark is a poor shelter.

# The Debris Hut
tags: shelter, debris hut, insulation, primitive, cold

A one-person shelter made of sticks and leaves that keeps you warm with body heat alone. It needs no tools and no fire.

## Build

1. **Ridgepole:** a sturdy pole about one and a half times your height. Prop one end at hip height on a stump or forked stick; the other end rests on the ground.
2. **Size check:** lie beneath it. It should clear your shoulders and feet by a hand's width, no more. Small is warm.
3. **Ribs:** lean sticks along both sides, close together, at a steep angle so rain sheds.
4. **Lattice:** lay fine brush over the ribs to stop debris falling through.
5. **Debris:** pile dry leaves, grass, ferns or pine needles over everything, **arm's length thick** (60 to 90 cm). More in real cold.
6. **Bed:** stuff the inside with the driest debris, enough that it compresses to at least 10 cm beneath you. The ground steals more heat than the air does.
7. **Door:** pull a pile of debris or a stuffed bag in after you to plug the entrance.
8. Lay a few branches on top to stop wind stripping the pile.

## Notes

- Building takes two to three hours. Start early.
- Wet debris still insulates, though less well. Put the driest material nearest your body.
- Do not light a fire inside or close by: the whole structure is tinder.

# Tarp Shelters
tags: shelter, tarp, ridgeline, a-frame, lean-to, rain

A 3 x 3 m tarp and 15 m of cord make a shelter in minutes. Three pitches cover most conditions.

## A-frame

Tie a ridgeline between two trees at chest height. Drape the tarp over it and stake the four corners. Sheds rain and wind from both sides. Pitch it lower in storms.

## Lean-to

Tie one edge high, stake the opposite edge to the ground, back to the wind. Open and roomy, and it reflects the heat of a fire built in front. Poor in shifting wind.

## Plow point

Tie one corner to a tree at head height and stake the opposite corner and the two sides to the ground. Fast, uses one anchor, and sheds wind well.

## Details that matter

- Pitch taut. Flapping fabric loses heat and wears through.
- Angle steeply in rain and snow so nothing pools.
- Tie a short drip line on the ridgeline just outside the tarp so water running down the cord drips off before reaching you.
- Use a taut-line hitch on the guy lines so you can re-tension without retying.
- No stakes? Tie off to rocks, logs or buried sticks.
- Insulate beneath you with a pad, boughs or dry leaves. A tarp stops rain, not ground cold.

# Staying Warm
tags: cold, insulation, layers, clothing, snow shelter, quinzhee

Heat is lost by conduction to the ground, convection to wind, radiation, and evaporation of sweat. Block each in turn.

## Clothing: COLD

- **C**lean: dirt and oil flatten insulation.
- **O**verheating avoided: sweat soaks your layers. Open up or strip a layer before hard work.
- **L**oose layers: trapped air is the insulation. Tight boots mean cold feet.
- **D**ry: wet clothing loses most of its value. Wool and synthetics keep some warmth wet; cotton does not.

## Practical habits

- Cover the head and neck.
- Sit and sleep on insulation, never on bare ground, snow or rock.
- Eat fat and sugar before sleep. Digestion makes heat.
- A bottle of hot water in the sleeping bag works for hours.
- Go to bed warm. Do star jumps first; a bag only holds the heat you bring.
- Empty your bladder. The body spends heat keeping urine warm.

## Snow shelter (quinzhee)

1. Pile snow 2 m high and 3 m across. Pack it as you go.
2. Push 30 cm sticks into the mound all over as thickness gauges.
3. Let it harden for one to two hours.
4. Tunnel in from the downwind side and hollow it out, stopping when you reach the stick ends.
5. Poke a fist-sized vent in the roof and keep it clear.
6. Raise the sleeping platform above the entrance so cold air drains out.

> **WARNING** Always keep a ventilation hole open in any snow shelter, and never burn a stove inside without one.
