# Foraging Principles
tags: foraging, wild food, plants, edible, poisonous, identification

Wild plants can add vitamins and calories, and they can kill. The skill is identification, not bravery.

## Rules

1. **Eat nothing you cannot name with certainty.** Confirm with several features: leaf, stem, flower, root, smell and habitat.
2. Learn a few common plants thoroughly rather than many plants vaguely.
3. Learn the deadly look-alikes of each plant before you learn the plant.
4. Gather away from roads, sprayed fields and polluted water.
5. Try a small amount of any new food first and wait a day.

## Myths that kill

- "If animals eat it, it is safe." False. Birds and deer eat things that poison humans.
- "Cooking destroys the poison." Often false.
- "It tastes fine, so it is fine." Some of the deadliest plants and fungi taste pleasant.

## Avoid unless expert

- All wild **mushrooms**. They offer few calories and the errors are fatal.
- Plants with umbrella-shaped flower heads that resemble wild carrot or parsley. This family includes hemlock.
- White or yellow berries, milky sap, and anything that smells of bitter almonds.

## Widely recognised starters

| Plant | Part | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Dandelion | Leaves, flowers, roots | Bitter, best young |
| Stinging nettle | Young tops | Cook to remove the sting |
| Cattail (bulrush) | Shoots, rhizome starch | Gather from clean water |
| Broadleaf plantain | Young leaves | Also used as a poultice |
| Acorns | Nut meat | Must be leached in changes of water until no longer bitter |

Confirm each one locally with a regional field guide before relying on it.

> **WARNING** The so-called universal edibility test is slow, does not detect many toxins, and must never be used on mushrooms. Treat it as a last resort only.

# Drying, Salting and Smoking
tags: preservation, drying, jerky, salt, smoking, dehydrate, meat, fish

Bacteria and mould need water. Take the water away and food keeps for months without refrigeration.

## Drying

- Slice thin and even, about 5 mm.
- Dry in moving warm air, 55 to 60 °C, or in sun and wind on racks covered with netting against flies.
- **Fruit** is done when leathery with no moist spots. **Vegetables** are done when brittle. Blanch most vegetables briefly first.
- Store airtight, cool and dark. Check after a week: any condensation means it needs more drying.

## Jerky

1. Use lean meat. Fat goes rancid. Trim it all off.
2. Slice 5 mm thick, across the grain for tender, with it for chewy.
3. Salt or marinate for several hours.
4. Dry until a strip cracks when bent but does not snap.
5. For safety, heat the meat to 71 °C (160 °F) at the start or end of drying.

## Dry salting

Pack fish or meat in layers with coarse salt, covering every surface. Salt draws out the moisture as brine; drain it. Heavily salted food keeps for months and must be soaked in fresh water before eating.

## Smoking

- **Hot smoking** (65 to 80 °C) cooks and flavours. The result keeps only days without cold.
- **Cold smoking** (below 30 °C) dries and flavours over days. It preserves only when combined with salting.
- Burn hardwood such as oak, beech, hickory, apple or alder. Never burn conifers, painted or treated wood.

# Fermentation
tags: preservation, fermentation, sauerkraut, pickles, salt, brine, vegetables

Salt and time turn fresh vegetables into food that keeps for months and gains vitamins. No heat or power is needed.

## Sauerkraut

1. Shred cabbage finely. Weigh it.
2. Add **2% salt by weight**: 20 g per kilogram of cabbage. Use salt without iodine or anti-caking agents.
3. Knead and squeeze for 5 to 10 minutes until it releases a pool of brine.
4. Pack tightly into a clean jar or crock, pressing out air pockets.
5. Weigh it down so the cabbage stays **under the brine**. This is the one rule that matters.
6. Cover loosely so gas can escape. Keep at 18 to 22 °C.
7. Taste from one week. It is ready at one to four weeks, when pleasantly sour. Then store cold.

## Brine pickles

For whole vegetables such as cucumbers, carrots and beans, cover with a 3.5 to 5% brine: 35 to 50 g of salt per litre of water. Keep submerged and ferment as above.

## Good or bad?

| Sign | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Bubbles, cloudy brine, sour smell | Normal |
| Flat white film on the surface | Kahm yeast. Harmless. Skim it off. |
| Fuzzy or coloured mould, slime, rotten smell | Discard |

# Canning Safely
tags: preservation, canning, botulism, pressure canner, jars, safety

Canning stores food for years, and done wrongly it produces botulism, which is odourless and often fatal. The method must match the food.

## Two methods

**Boiling-water bath** is safe only for high-acid food (pH 4.6 or below): most fruit, jams, pickles in vinegar, and tomatoes with added lemon juice or citric acid.

**Pressure canning** is required for every low-acid food: vegetables, beans, meat, fish, poultry, soups and stock. Botulism spores survive boiling. Only 116 °C (240 °F), reached under pressure, destroys them.

## Practice

1. Follow a tested recipe exactly for jar size, headspace, time and pressure.
2. Raise the pressure or time for altitude as the recipe directs.
3. Use sound jars and new lids. Wipe rims clean before sealing.
4. After cooling 12 to 24 hours, check each seal: the lid should be concave and should not flex.
5. Label with contents and date. Store cool and dark. Use within a year for best quality.

> **WARNING** Never taste food from a jar that is bulging, leaking, spurting on opening, mouldy or foul-smelling. Dispose of it where people and animals cannot reach it. If in doubt about home-canned low-acid food, boil it for 10 minutes before eating.

# Bread Without a Shop
tags: bread, sourdough, bannock, hardtack, baking, flour, yeast

Flour stores for a long time. Knowing three breads means you can use it without packaged yeast, an oven, or either.

## Sourdough starter

Wild yeast lives on flour. To capture it:

1. Mix 50 g flour and 50 g water in a jar. Cover loosely.
2. Each day, discard half and feed with another 50 g of each.
3. In 5 to 7 days it should double within hours of feeding and smell pleasantly sour. It is now alive and will last indefinitely if fed.

## Basic sourdough loaf

500 g flour, 350 g water, 100 g active starter, 10 g salt. Mix, rest 30 minutes, fold a few times over 4 hours, shape, prove until puffy, and bake hot (230 °C) in a covered pot for 30 minutes, then 15 uncovered.

## Bannock (no yeast, no oven)

2 cups flour, 2 teaspoons baking powder, half a teaspoon of salt, 2 tablespoons of fat, and water to make a stiff dough. Fry in a pan as a flat cake, or wrap around a green stick and bake over coals.

## Hardtack (keeps for years)

3 cups flour, 1 cup water, 2 teaspoons salt. Knead stiff, roll 1 cm thick, cut into squares and prick holes through. Bake at 190 °C for 30 minutes a side, then dry in a cooling oven. Stored dry it lasts for years. Soak or dunk before eating.
