# Map and Compass
tags: navigation, compass, map, bearing, declination, triangulation

A paper map and a baseplate compass need no batteries and no signal. Three skills cover nearly everything.

## Taking a bearing from the map

1. Lay the compass edge along the line from where you are to where you are going, direction arrow pointing at the destination.
2. Turn the dial until its grid lines run parallel with the map's north-south lines, north to north.
3. Read the bearing at the index mark. This is a **true** (grid) bearing.
4. Correct for declination, below.
5. Hold the compass level, turn your body until the red needle sits inside the orienting arrow ("red in the shed"), and walk where the direction arrow points.

## Declination

Magnetic north and true north differ by an angle printed on the map margin. It changes with place and slowly with time.

- **Map to compass:** subtract east declination, add west. *East is least, west is best.*
- **Compass to map:** do the opposite.

Formula: magnetic bearing = true bearing minus declination, with east counted as positive.

## Fixing your position (resection)

1. Identify two or three landmarks that are on the map.
2. Take a compass bearing to each and convert it to true.
3. From each landmark on the map, draw a line along the back bearing (bearing plus or minus 180).
4. You are where the lines cross.

## Travel techniques

- **Handrail:** follow a line feature such as a stream, ridge or track.
- **Catching feature:** note something large beyond your target that tells you if you have overshot.
- **Aiming off:** aim deliberately to one side of a target on a line feature, so you know which way to turn when you reach the line.

# Direction From Sun and Stars
tags: navigation, sun, stars, polaris, southern cross, shadow stick, direction

When there is no compass, the sky still gives direction. These methods are approximate: good to within ten or twenty degrees.

## Shadow stick (works anywhere the sun casts a shadow)

1. Push a straight stick upright into level ground.
2. Mark the tip of its shadow with a stone.
3. Wait 15 to 30 minutes and mark the tip again.
4. The first mark is **west**, the second is **east**. A line across them at right angles runs north-south.

## Watch method

Use an analogue watch set to standard time, not daylight saving.

- **Northern hemisphere:** point the hour hand at the sun. South lies halfway between the hour hand and 12.
- **Southern hemisphere:** point 12 at the sun. North lies halfway between 12 and the hour hand.

Unreliable in the tropics.

## North Star (northern hemisphere)

Find the Big Dipper. Follow the two stars at the outer edge of its bowl upward, five times the distance between them, to a moderately bright star on its own. That is Polaris, within a degree of true north. Its height above the horizon in degrees equals your latitude.

## Southern Cross (southern hemisphere)

Extend the long axis of the cross four and a half times its length from the foot. That point is the south celestial pole. Drop straight down to the horizon for south.

## Anywhere

- The sun rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west, exactly so only at the equinoxes.
- Orion's belt rises very nearly due east and sets very nearly due west.

# Dead Reckoning and Pace Count
tags: navigation, pace count, distance, dead reckoning, naismith, travel time

Knowing how far you have gone, on what bearing, tells you where you are without seeing a single landmark.

## Find your pace count

1. Measure 100 m on flat ground.
2. Walk it normally, counting every time your **left** foot lands.
3. Repeat three times and average. Most adults count 60 to 70.

Recount with a loaded pack. Add roughly 10 to 20% uphill, in soft ground, in darkness or when tired.

## Keeping count

Tie nine knots or slide nine beads on a cord: move one every 100 m. After the ninth, the next 100 m is a kilometre: move a second-row bead and reset. Pebbles moved from one pocket to another work as well.

## Dead reckoning

1. Mark your start on the map.
2. For each leg, write down the bearing and the distance walked.
3. Plot each leg in turn. The end of the last is your estimated position.

Errors add up. Correct your position whenever you reach an identifiable feature.

## Estimating time

Naismith's rule: allow **1 hour for every 5 km**, plus **1 hour for every 600 m of climb**. Add a quarter to a half for heavy packs, rough ground or a tired group.

## Avoiding drift

People without a reference walk in circles. Pick a landmark on your bearing, walk to it, then pick the next. In featureless ground, send a partner ahead on the bearing and walk to them.
