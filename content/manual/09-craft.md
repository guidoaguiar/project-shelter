# Eight Essential Knots
tags: knots, rope, cordage, bowline, hitch, lashing

Eight knots cover shelter, hauling, joining and rescue. Practise until you can tie each in the dark with cold hands.

## The set

| Knot | Job | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Bowline | Fixed loop that will not slip | Rescue loop, anchor to a tree; unties after heavy load |
| Clove hitch | Quick attachment to a pole | Starts and ends lashings |
| Two half hitches | Secures a line to a post or ring | Simple, holds under steady pull |
| Taut-line hitch | Adjustable loop | Tent and tarp guy lines |
| Trucker's hitch | Pulley-like tensioning | Tight ridgelines, lashing loads |
| Sheet bend | Joins two ropes, even of unequal thickness | The correct joining knot |
| Figure eight | Stopper | Keeps a rope from running out of a hole or device |
| Prusik | Friction loop that grips when loaded | Climbing a rope, adjustable tie-outs |

## Tying the three you need most

**Bowline:** make a small loop in the standing line. Pass the end up through the loop, around behind the standing line, and back down through the loop. Tighten.

**Taut-line hitch:** pass the end around the anchor. Wrap it twice around the standing line, working back toward the anchor, then once more around the standing line on the far side of those wraps. Slide to tension.

**Trucker's hitch:** tie a slip loop in the standing line. Pass the end around the anchor and up through the loop. Pull down hard, pinch, and finish with two half hitches.

## Rules

- The reef (square) knot is for tying bundles and bandages. Never use it to join ropes that will take load.
- Dress every knot neatly and pull it snug before trusting it.
- Leave a tail at least ten rope diameters long.

# Making Cordage
tags: cordage, rope, fibre, reverse wrap, natural materials, string

Cord ties shelters, sets lines, repairs gear and makes bowstrings. It can be made from plants found almost anywhere.

## Fibres

- **Stalks:** stinging nettle, dogbane, milkweed, flax, hemp. Gather when dry and dead, crush, and peel the fibre from the woody core.
- **Inner bark:** basswood (lime), cedar, willow, elm. Strip from dead branches or soak fresh bark until the layers separate.
- **Leaves:** yucca, agave, cattail. Scrape or pound away the pulp.
- **Animal:** sinew and rawhide, strong but weak when wet.

## Reverse wrap (two-ply twist)

1. Take a bundle of fibre about half as thick as the cord you want.
2. Hold it a third of the way along and twist until it kinks into a loop. Pinch the loop.
3. You now have two strands. Twist the far strand **away** from you.
4. Bring it **toward** you over the near strand. The strands have swapped places.
5. Repeat: twist away, wrap toward.

The opposing twists lock against each other, which is what keeps the cord from unravelling.

## Splicing in

When a strand thins, lay new fibre alongside it and keep twisting. Because you started a third of the way along, the two strands run out at different points, so splices never coincide. Staggered splices keep the cord strong.

## Stronger rope

Twist three two-ply cords together in the opposite direction. Test every length by pulling hard before trusting it.

# Sharpening Edges
tags: knife, axe, sharpening, edge, whetstone, strop, tools

A dull blade needs more force, slips more easily and causes more injuries than a sharp one. Sharpening is a matter of a steady angle.

## Angle

- About **20 degrees a side** for a general knife.
- About **25 degrees** for axes and heavy choppers.
- Thinner for fine slicing, at the cost of durability.

Holding the same angle throughout matters more than the exact number.

## Method

1. Start on the coarse stone if the edge is damaged or very dull. Wet it with water or oil as the stone requires.
2. Push the edge along the stone as if slicing a thin layer off it, from heel to tip.
3. Continue on one side until you feel a **burr**, a thin wire of metal along the whole of the opposite side.
4. Switch sides and repeat until the burr flips.
5. Move to the fine stone with lighter strokes, alternating sides.
6. Strop on leather, a belt or cardboard, dragging the edge backward, to remove the burr.

## Testing

A sharp edge slices paper cleanly and catches on a thumbnail rather than skating. Light reflecting from the edge marks a dull spot.

## Field substitutes

A smooth river stone, the unglazed ring on the base of a ceramic mug, or fine sandpaper on a flat stick.

## Axes

Use a file for nicks, working from the cheek toward the edge, then a round stone in small circles. Wear gloves and keep fingers behind the edge.

# Soap From Fat and Lye
tags: soap, lye, fat, tallow, hygiene, saponification

Soap is fat reacted with a strong alkali. The method is old and simple, and the lye deserves respect.

## Measured method (sodium hydroxide)

A basic batch: **1000 g lard, about 132 g sodium hydroxide, 330 g water.** Other fats need different amounts: check a lye table for each fat and measure by weight.

1. Wear goggles and gloves. Work with ventilation.
2. Add the **lye to the water**, never water to lye. It heats sharply. Let it cool to about 40 °C.
3. Melt the fat and cool to about the same temperature.
4. Pour the lye solution into the fat and stir until it thickens like custard and a drizzle leaves a trace on the surface.
5. Pour into a mould, cover and leave for a day or two.
6. Cut into bars and **cure for 4 to 6 weeks** on a rack, turning occasionally.

## Wood-ash method

Hardwood ash leached with rainwater yields potassium lye, which makes a soft soap.

1. Fill a container with a drain hole with white hardwood ash over a layer of straw or gravel.
2. Pour rainwater through slowly and collect the brown liquid.
3. Simmer it with rendered fat for several hours, stirring, until it thickens.

The strength of ash lye varies, so results are unpredictable. Expect to adjust by adding more fat or more lye.

> **WARNING** Lye causes severe burns and blindness. Keep vinegar out of the treatment: flush any contact with large amounts of running water for 15 minutes.

# Light Without Power
tags: light, candle, oil lamp, tallow, lantern, blackout

Simple lamps can be made from kitchen materials when the batteries run down.

## Oil lamp

1. Half fill a glass jar with any cooking oil.
2. Twist a strip of cotton (string, shoelace, a strip of old shirt) into a wick.
3. Hold it upright with a piece of wire bent over the rim, or thread it through a hole in a metal lid, with about 5 mm above the oil.
4. Let the wick soak, then light it.

Olive oil burns cleanest. A short wick gives a small steady flame; a long one smokes. Cooking oil does not flare if spilled, which makes it safer than paraffin.

## Tallow candles

Render beef or mutton fat by simmering in water and skimming the clean fat when cool. Dip a cotton wick repeatedly, letting each layer set, or pour the fat into a jar around a wick. Tallow smokes and smells; beeswax, if you have it, does not.

## More light from less

- Stand the flame in front of a mirror or foil.
- A headlamp strapped to a full water jug, beam inward, lights a room softly.

## Safety

Stand lamps on a stable non-flammable surface, away from curtains, and never leave them burning unattended or while you sleep.
