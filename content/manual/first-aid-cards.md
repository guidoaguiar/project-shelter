# CPR
tags: card, first aid, cpr, cardiac arrest, not breathing, unconscious, aed, chest compressions

**Call emergency services if you can.** Use this for a person who is unresponsive and not breathing normally, or only gasping.

1. Make sure the scene is safe. Tap the person and shout.
2. Call emergency services, or send someone to call and to fetch an AED (defibrillator).
3. Put the heel of one hand on the centre of the chest, the other hand on top.
4. Push **hard and fast**: at least 5 cm (2 in) but not more than 6 cm (2.4 in), 100 to 120 times a minute. Let the chest rise fully after each push.
5. If you are trained, give 2 rescue breaths after every 30 pushes. If not, keep pushing without stopping (hands-only CPR).
6. Turn the AED on as soon as it arrives and follow its voice prompts.
7. Keep going until help takes over, the person starts breathing normally, or you cannot go on.

**Children and babies:** for a child, use one or two hands and push about 5 cm (2 in). For a baby under 1, use two fingers and push about 4 cm (1.5 in). If you are alone with no phone, give about 2 minutes of CPR before leaving to call.

**Do not**
- Stop to check for a pulse unless you are trained to.
- Worry about hurting the person: without CPR they will die.

**Get help urgently if**
- Always. CPR keeps blood moving only until help arrives.

Sources: MedlinePlus, CPR (US National Library of Medicine, public domain); Mayo Clinic, Cardiopulmonary resuscitation: first aid.

# Choking
tags: card, first aid, choking, airway, back blows, abdominal thrusts, heimlich

**Call emergency services if you can.** Use this when someone cannot breathe, speak or cough because something is stuck in the throat.

1. Ask: "Are you choking?" If they can cough strongly, encourage them to keep coughing.
2. If they cannot breathe, speak or cough, stand behind them and lean them forward.
3. Give up to **5 back blows** between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
4. If that does not clear it, give up to **5 abdominal thrusts**: make a fist just above the navel, grasp it with the other hand, and pull sharply in and up.
5. Keep alternating 5 back blows and 5 thrusts until the object comes out.
6. If the person becomes unresponsive, lower them to the ground and start CPR.

**Babies under 1:** lay the baby face down along your forearm, head lower than the body, and give 5 back blows. Then turn them face up and give 5 chest thrusts with two fingers in the centre of the chest.

**Do not**
- Give abdominal thrusts to a baby under 1. Use chest thrusts for pregnant or very large people.
- Sweep blindly in the mouth with your fingers.

**Get help urgently if**
- Abdominal thrusts were given: the person must be checked for internal injury.
- The object did not come out.

Sources: NHS, Choking (UK Open Government Licence); MedlinePlus, Abdominal thrusts (public domain).

# Severe Bleeding
tags: card, first aid, bleeding, haemorrhage, tourniquet, pressure, wound

**Call emergency services if you can.** Blood that spurts, pools or will not stop can kill in minutes.

1. Protect yourself with gloves or a plastic bag if you can, but do not delay.
2. Find where the blood is coming from.
3. Press hard on the wound with a cloth or gauze, using both hands. Do not lift to check.
4. Pack deep wounds with gauze or clean cloth, then press on top.
5. For life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg, apply a tourniquet 5 to 8 cm above the wound and tighten until the bleeding stops. Note the time.
6. Keep the person lying down and warm.

**Do not**
- Remove soaked dressings. Add more on top.
- Loosen a tourniquet once it is on.

**Get help urgently if**
- Always, for bleeding you could not stop with pressure, or when you used a tourniquet.

The full steps are in Stopping Severe Bleeding under MEDICAL.

Sources: US Army FM 3-05.70 (public domain); Stop the Bleed (US Department of Homeland Security, public domain).

# Burns
tags: card, first aid, burns, scald, fire, cool water

**Call emergency services if you can** for serious burns. Cooling quickly limits the damage.

1. Get the person away from the heat source. Stop, drop and roll if clothing is on fire.
2. Cool the burn under cool running water for **20 minutes** if you can, and for at least 10.
3. Remove rings, watches and clothing near the burn, unless they are stuck to the skin.
4. Cover loosely with cling film or a clean, non-fluffy cloth.
5. Keep the rest of the person warm: cooling large burns can cause hypothermia.

**Do not**
- Use ice, iced water, butter, oil or creams.
- Break blisters or pull off stuck clothing.

**Get help urgently if**
- The burn is larger than the person's hand, or deep, white or charred.
- It is on the face, hands, feet, groin or a joint, or goes all the way round a limb.
- It is electrical or chemical, or the person breathed smoke.
- The person is a child or an older adult.

Sources: NHS, Burns and scalds (UK Open Government Licence); MedlinePlus, Burns (public domain).

# Shock
tags: card, first aid, shock, blood loss, pale, clammy, faint

**Call emergency services if you can.** Shock means the body is not getting enough blood flow. It follows heavy bleeding, burns, severe infection or allergy.

Signs: pale, cold, clammy skin; fast, weak pulse; fast breathing; confusion, thirst or drowsiness.

1. Treat the cause you can see first, such as bleeding.
2. Lay the person down flat.
3. If there is no injury to the head, neck, back or legs, raise the legs about 30 cm (12 in).
4. Loosen tight clothing and keep them warm with a blanket.
5. Watch their breathing. If they vomit, turn them onto their side.

**Do not**
- Give anything to eat or drink.
- Leave them alone.

**Get help urgently if**
- Always. Shock is life-threatening.

Source: MedlinePlus, Shock (public domain).

# Severe Allergic Reaction
tags: card, first aid, anaphylaxis, allergy, allergic reaction, epinephrine, adrenaline, auto-injector, epipen

**Call emergency services if you can.** Anaphylaxis can close the airway within minutes.

Signs: trouble breathing or swallowing, swelling of the face, lips or tongue, hives, dizziness or collapse, often after food, a sting or a medicine.

1. If they have an adrenaline (epinephrine) auto-injector, **use it now**: into the outer thigh, through clothing if needed, following the instructions on the device.
2. Call emergency services and say "anaphylaxis".
3. Lie them down with legs raised. If breathing is hard, let them sit up instead.
4. If there is no improvement after 5 minutes and a second auto-injector is available, use it.
5. If they stop breathing normally, start CPR.

**Do not**
- Wait to see whether it gets better.
- Let them stand up or walk, even if they feel better.

**Get help urgently if**
- Always, even after the injection works: symptoms can come back.

Sources: NHS, Anaphylaxis (UK Open Government Licence); MedlinePlus, Anaphylaxis (public domain).

# Stroke
tags: card, first aid, stroke, fast, face, arm, speech, brain

**Call emergency services if you can.** Every minute counts: treatment works best within hours.

Think **FAST**:
- **Face:** ask them to smile. Does one side droop?
- **Arms:** ask them to raise both arms. Does one drift down?
- **Speech:** ask them to repeat a simple phrase. Is it slurred or strange?
- **Time:** if you see any of these, call now.

1. Note the time the symptoms started. Doctors need it.
2. Keep the person comfortable and still.
3. If they become unresponsive but are breathing, turn them onto their side.

**Do not**
- Give food, drink or medicine, including aspirin.
- Let them sleep it off or drive themselves.

**Get help urgently if**
- Always, even if the signs go away.

Source: CDC, Stroke Signs and Symptoms (public domain).

# Seizure
tags: card, first aid, seizure, epilepsy, fit, convulsion

**Call emergency services if you can** when a seizure is long or unusual. Most seizures stop on their own within a few minutes.

1. Stay with the person and **time the seizure**.
2. Move hard or sharp objects away and cushion the head.
3. Loosen anything tight around the neck.
4. When the jerking stops, turn them onto their side to keep the airway clear.
5. Stay until they are fully awake, and speak calmly.

**Do not**
- Hold the person down.
- Put anything in their mouth.

**Get help urgently if**
- The seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes, or another one follows.
- The person does not wake up, has trouble breathing, is injured, pregnant or in water.
- It is their first seizure.

Source: CDC, First Aid for Seizures (public domain).

# Hypothermia
tags: card, first aid, hypothermia, cold, shivering, exposure, rewarming

**Call emergency services if you can.** Hypothermia sets in when the body loses heat faster than it makes it, even in cool wet wind.

Signs: shivering, confusion, slurred speech, clumsiness, drowsiness. Shivering may stop as it gets worse.

1. Get the person out of the cold and wind.
2. Replace wet clothing with dry clothing.
3. Warm the **centre** of the body first: chest, neck, head and groin, with blankets, a sleeping bag or skin-to-skin contact.
4. If they are fully awake and can swallow, give warm, sweet drinks.
5. Handle them gently. If they stop breathing, start CPR.

**Do not**
- Rub the arms and legs, or put them in a hot bath.
- Give alcohol.

**Get help urgently if**
- The person is confused, drowsy or unresponsive, or has stopped shivering.

Sources: CDC, Hypothermia (public domain); US Army FM 3-05.70 (public domain).

# Heat Stroke
tags: card, first aid, heat stroke, heat, hyperthermia, cooling, heat exhaustion

**Call emergency services if you can.** Heat stroke is an emergency: the body can no longer cool itself.

Signs: very high body temperature, hot red skin (dry or damp), confusion, fast strong pulse, headache, collapse.

1. Move the person to a cool, shaded place.
2. Cool them **fast**: put them in cold water up to the neck if you can. Otherwise soak them with cold water and fan them, and put ice packs or cold wet cloths on the neck, armpits and groin.
3. Remove extra clothing.
4. Keep cooling until help arrives or they are no longer confused.

**Do not**
- Give drinks unless they are fully alert and can swallow.
- Give fever medicines such as paracetamol or aspirin: they do not help.

**Get help urgently if**
- Always for heat stroke. For heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, weakness, nausea), get help if symptoms get worse or last more than an hour.

Sources: CDC / NIOSH, Heat-related Illnesses (public domain); American Red Cross, Heat Stroke.
